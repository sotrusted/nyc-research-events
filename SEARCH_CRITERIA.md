# Search criteria

## Mission
Discover **high-signal, technically substantive NYC research events**, especially where mathematics intersects with AI. Prioritize opportunities to learn from active researchers and ask meaningful questions, not generic networking.

## Geography and access
- Primary: in-person NYC (NYU Courant/CDS/CS, Columbia, Flatiron, CUNY Graduate Center, Simons Foundation).
- Secondary: Princeton/IAS and nearby institutions only for exceptional, accessible events; label travel burden.
- Verify whether events are public, NYU-only, registration-only, waitlisted, or closed. Never assume access.
- Online talks only when exceptional and with substantive technical materials.

## Topic priority
**Highest:** mathematical foundations of ML, AI for mathematics and scientific discovery, reasoning and formal verification, theorem proving, RL and agentic systems, generalization, evaluations, optimization and probability in modern AI.

**High:** theoretical CS, statistics, information theory, interpretability, robustness, computational math, scientific ML, dynamical systems, robotics and embodied learning.

**Selective:** pure math (including geometry, algebra, logic, groups), physics/computation, and interdisciplinary research where the speaker or question is unusually compelling.

## Include
- Research seminars, colloquia, technical workshops, serious panels/debates/town halls.
- Active researchers presenting original results, open problems, methods, or a rigorous conceptual dispute.
- Significant TBA talks as provisional leads if the speaker/series is strong.
- Events with meaningful audience Q&A, reading materials, or researcher interaction.

## Exclude by default
- Startup pitches, product demos, investor panels, recruiting, sponsored promotion.
- Generic AI networking and hype-driven future-of-AI discussions without technical substance.
- Beginner workshops, broad career panels, undergraduate review, repetitive community meetups.
- Events that are closed or impractical to attend, unless recordings or papers are valuable.

## Scoring rubric (0–3 per category, total /15)
1. Research depth and specificity.
2. Fit with mathematical AI, agents, reasoning, theory, or adjacent math.
3. Speaker originality and direct research involvement.
4. Access, Q&A, and opportunity to interact.
5. Novelty, timeliness, and scarcity of the opportunity.

12–15: prioritize attendance; 9–11: recommend if feasible; 6–8: track/read; 0–5: normally omit. Scores are editorial judgments, not objective rankings.

## Search procedure
1. Search forward 4–8 weeks, preferably weekly, using organizer calendars and seminar series pages.
2. Queries include: `NYU Courant AI math seminar`, `Columbia ML theory colloquium`, `Flatiron scientific machine learning seminar`, `NYC theorem proving research talk`, `site:cims.nyu.edu mathematics AI events`.
3. Verify speaker, affiliation, title, abstract, date, time (America/New_York), room, admission, registration, and original URL. Leave unknowns blank.
4. Deduplicate by date, speaker, and host. Distinguish tentative from confirmed.
5. Look for papers, slides, recordings, and relevant background reading.
6. Recheck shortlisted events 48–72 hours beforehand.

## Change alerts
Monitor newly announced speakers, dates, titles/topics, rooms/times, cancellations, access or registration changes, panels/debates/town halls, materials/links, and meaningful shifts in series goals or scope.

Notify only for **substantive changes**. State previous → current, evidence/source, when checked, and why attending or reading is worthwhile. Do not alert on cosmetic formatting or unchanged TBA. If nothing material changed, remain silent.

## Editorial preferences
Prefer deep research over marketing, mathematical rigor over generic AI commentary, and unusual debates or opportunities to speak with researchers. Avoid treating all AI events as equally interesting.
